# Why GRC Teams Struggle to Close Audit Findings: The Real Problem Behind Remediation Delays

In Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), identifying a control gap is only the first step. The real challenge begins when an audit finding must be remediated, validated, and formally closed.

Many organizations conduct internal audits, regulatory assessments, and information security reviews, yet the same findings continue to appear in subsequent audit cycles. Why does this happen?

The problem is often not a lack of policies or procedures. It is the gap between identifying a weakness and implementing a sustainable solution.

## 1. Unclear ownership

One of the most common challenges is the absence of a clearly accountable control owner. The GRC team identifies an issue, but the remediation depends on IT, application teams, information security, or a third-party vendor.

Without a defined owner and an agreed deadline, remediation can remain pending for weeks or months.

**Solution:** Assign a named owner, define a target completion date, and establish escalation procedures for overdue findings.

## 2. Weak root cause analysis

Organizations sometimes resolve the immediate symptom without addressing the underlying problem.

For example, an audit identifies missing access reviews. The team completes a one-time review, but no recurring process is established. The same finding may reappear during the next assessment.

**Solution:** Document the root cause, corrective action, preventive action, and evidence that demonstrates the control is operating effectively.

## 3. Inadequate remediation evidence

A remediation task may be marked complete without sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the control has been implemented.

A verbal confirmation or an email stating that an issue is resolved may not be enough for an auditor to validate the closure.

**Solution:** Define evidence requirements upfront. Depending on the finding, evidence could include approved procedures, system configurations, access review records, relevant logs, or documented management approvals.

## 4. Poor risk-based prioritization

Not every finding carries the same level of risk. Treating all observations equally can cause teams to focus on easy, low-impact issues while more significant weaknesses remain unresolved.

**Solution:** Prioritize findings based on business impact, likelihood, regulatory exposure, data sensitivity, and the effectiveness of existing controls.

## 5. Lack of continuous monitoring

A control may be effective when tested but deteriorate over time because ownership changes, systems are modified, or periodic reviews are missed.

**Solution:** Establish recurring control testing, remediation tracking, and management reporting to identify deteriorating controls before the next audit.

## A practical GRC remediation framework

A structured remediation lifecycle can make audit closure more effective:

1. Record the finding and its associated risk.
2. Assign an accountable owner and target date.
3. Perform root cause analysis.
4. Agree on corrective and preventive actions.
5. Collect and review supporting evidence.
6. Validate implementation and operating effectiveness where applicable.
7. Close the finding with documented approval.
8. Monitor the control to reduce recurrence.

## Key performance indicators for GRC teams

Organizations can monitor remediation effectiveness using metrics such as:

- Percentage of findings closed within the agreed timeline.
- Number of overdue high-risk findings.
- Average time taken to remediate findings.
- Percentage of findings recurring across audit cycles.
- Percentage of closures rejected because evidence was insufficient.

These metrics should be interpreted alongside risk severity and the quality of remediation, rather than used as standalone measures of success.

## Conclusion

Effective GRC is not about achieving a clean audit report alone. It is about ensuring that identified weaknesses are addressed, risks are reduced to acceptable levels, and controls remain effective over time.

The true measure of a mature GRC function is not how many findings it identifies, but how effectively the organization resolves them and prevents recurrence.
