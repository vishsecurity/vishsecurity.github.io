# Running GRC in a Toxic Work Culture: When Risk Management Becomes a People Challenge

Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) is often discussed in terms of policies, controls, audits, regulatory requirements, and risk registers.

But what happens when the biggest challenge is not the framework, but the work culture?

Imagine a workplace where employees hesitate to report control failures, managers resist audit observations, remediation tasks are repeatedly delayed, responsibilities are unclear, and raising a risk is treated as criticism rather than an opportunity to improve.

In such an environment, implementing GRC becomes much more difficult.

**The challenge is no longer just identifying risk. It is creating accountability in an environment where transparency may not be encouraged.**

## 1. When people avoid reporting risks

In a healthy control environment, employees can raise concerns without fear of retaliation. In a toxic environment, people may hide mistakes, delay reporting incidents, or avoid documenting weaknesses.

This creates a serious governance problem: management may believe controls are effective because problems are not being reported.

**How to handle it:**
- Establish clear, documented reporting channels.
- Use evidence-based risk assessments rather than personal opinions.
- Escalate material risks through defined governance processes.
- Encourage factual reporting and appropriate protection for people who raise concerns.

A lack of reported issues does not necessarily mean a lack of risk.

## 2. When managers resist audit findings

A GRC professional may identify a genuine control gap, only to face resistance from a stakeholder who does not want the observation recorded.

The discussion can become personal instead of focusing on the risk.

**How to handle it:**

Present every finding using a consistent structure:

- **Condition:** What was observed?
- **Criteria:** What requirement or control objective applies?
- **Risk:** What could happen if the gap remains unresolved?
- **Evidence:** What supports the observation?
- **Action:** What remediation is required?
- **Ownership:** Who is accountable, and by when?

For example, instead of saying, "The IT team is not following access management procedures," state:

"Evidence of the required periodic privileged-access review was not available for the assessment period. This limits our ability to confirm that privileged access remains appropriate. The control owner should provide the review records or implement an agreed corrective action."

The objective is to assess the control, not attack the person.

## 3. When accountability is missing

In some organizations, audit findings remain open because everyone is involved but nobody is accountable.

The GRC team repeatedly sends emails, attends meetings, and updates trackers, yet remediation does not progress.

**How to handle it:**

Maintain a structured audit finding and remediation tracker containing:

- Finding ID and risk rating
- Root cause
- Agreed corrective action
- Accountable owner
- Target completion date
- Current status and blockers
- Closure evidence
- Validation and approval

Use a defined escalation process for overdue high-risk findings. Where ownership or resources are disputed, record the issue and seek a management decision rather than allowing it to remain indefinitely unresolved.

## 4. When GRC becomes a blame game

GRC should not be used to blame departments, protect managers from accountability, or demonstrate that one team is more compliant than another.

Its purpose is to provide a reliable view of risk and help the organization improve its controls.

When a finding becomes personal, return the discussion to the control objective, available evidence, business impact, and remediation options.

Distinguish between an honest mistake, a poorly designed process, inadequate resources, and deliberate disregard for an important control. These situations may require different responses.

## 5. When management wants a clean audit report at any cost

One of the most difficult situations occurs when there is pressure to close findings without sufficient evidence, downgrade risks without justification, or accept incomplete remediation.

A clean report is not proof of a healthy control environment.

**A GRC professional must protect the integrity of risk reporting.**

Document the evidence, explain the consequences of premature closure, follow the approved risk acceptance and escalation process, and ensure that material limitations are communicated to the appropriate decision-makers.

Never fabricate evidence or represent an unverified control as effective.

## 6. Protect your professional credibility

Working in a difficult culture requires both technical discipline and professional judgment.

- Keep factual records of significant decisions and agreed actions.
- Communicate important risks through appropriate, authorized channels.
- Avoid emotional or accusatory language in audit documentation.
- Maintain confidentiality and follow internal reporting procedures.
- Set realistic expectations about what the GRC function can control.
- Seek support from appropriate leadership, HR, compliance, or independent reporting channels when there are serious concerns about retaliation or misconduct.

GRC professionals are responsible for providing accurate risk assessments and appropriate challenge. They cannot single-handedly repair an unhealthy organizational culture.

## 7. Measure whether GRC is actually working

Rather than measuring success only by the number of completed audits, monitor indicators that reveal the health of the control environment:

- Number and age of overdue high-risk findings
- Repeat audit observations
- Percentage of remediation closures supported by sufficient evidence
- Delays caused by unresolved ownership or resource issues
- Overdue risk acceptances and control exceptions
- Timeliness of incident and control-gap reporting
- Management decisions pending on material risks

Interpret these metrics in context. For example, an increase in reported issues may reflect improved transparency rather than deteriorating security.

## Final thoughts

Running GRC in a toxic work culture is challenging because effective governance depends on transparency, accountability, cooperation, and trust.

When these are missing, GRC professionals must rely on clear evidence, consistent processes, documented decisions, proportionate escalation, and professional integrity.

**You may not be able to change the culture immediately, but you can ensure that risks are assessed objectively, findings are documented accurately, and decisions are traceable.**

And one important lesson: if an organization repeatedly punishes people for raising legitimate risks, ignores material control failures, or pressures professionals to misrepresent compliance, the problem may be bigger than the GRC process itself.

Sometimes improving governance requires changing the process. Sometimes it requires leadership intervention. And sometimes, protecting your own professional integrity means recognizing that the environment is not sustainable.
