# Beyond Compliance: How to Integrate ISO 27001, ISO 27701, COSO, and CMMI into One Powerful GRC Operating Model

**By Vishal Chaudhary | Cybersecurity GRC | IT Risk | Audit & Regulatory Compliance**

## Introduction: Why GRC Needs More Than Compliance Checklists

Organizations today face a growing range of challenges, including cybersecurity threats, privacy breaches, regulatory requirements, third-party risks, and ineffective internal controls.

Many organizations implement ISO/IEC 27001 to manage information security and ISO/IEC 27701 to manage privacy. However, implementing standards alone does not automatically create a mature Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) function.

Common problems include:

- Policies exist, but business teams do not consistently follow them.
- Risk registers are maintained, but risks are not connected to business decisions.
- Controls are documented, but their operating effectiveness is not tested.
- Audit findings are closed on paper but recur in subsequent audits.
- Privacy requirements are identified, but personal data flows remain poorly understood.
- Management receives compliance reports without meaningful risk and performance indicators.

**The solution is to integrate ISO 27001, ISO 27701, COSO, and CMMI into a unified GRC operating model.**

Each framework addresses a different aspect of organizational performance. When used together, they help organizations move beyond passing audits toward managing risk, improving controls, protecting personal information, and achieving continuous improvement.

## 1. Understanding the Four Frameworks

### ISO/IEC 27001:2022 — Building an Information Security Management System

ISO 27001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

Its purpose is to help organizations manage information security risks systematically.

Key implementation activities include:

- Defining the ISMS scope.
- Identifying information assets and associated risks.
- Conducting information security risk assessments.
- Selecting and implementing appropriate controls.
- Maintaining the Statement of Applicability (SoA).
- Monitoring control effectiveness.
- Conducting internal audits and management reviews.
- Addressing nonconformities and corrective actions.

**Real-world example:**

A fintech company stores customer information in a cloud-based lending application. The GRC team identifies unauthorized access as a significant risk.

The team establishes access management procedures, periodic access reviews, logging requirements, and incident response processes. It then tests whether these controls operate effectively and retains evidence for audit.

ISO 27001 provides the management-system structure for managing this risk.

### ISO/IEC 27701:2025 — Integrating Privacy into GRC

ISO 27701 provides a framework for establishing and improving a Privacy Information Management System (PIMS).

The 2025 edition should be considered when designing a new privacy management program, while organizations with existing certifications or contractual obligations should confirm their applicable transition and certification requirements.

Privacy management requires more than protecting databases. Organizations must understand how personal information is collected, used, shared, retained, and deleted.

Key activities include:

- Maintaining an inventory of personal information processing.
- Identifying applicable privacy obligations.
- Defining responsibilities for personal information controllers and processors.
- Assessing privacy risks.
- Establishing retention and deletion requirements.
- Managing data subject requests.
- Assessing third-party privacy risks.
- Developing privacy incident and breach-handling procedures.

**Real-world example:**

A digital lending platform collects identity documents, bank statements, and contact information.

The GRC team maps the personal information lifecycle, documents processing purposes, evaluates access restrictions, reviews retention requirements, and establishes procedures for responding to privacy requests.

ISO 27701 complements information security by addressing privacy governance and the responsible handling of personal information.

### COSO — Connecting Risk and Internal Controls to Business Objectives

COSO's Internal Control—Integrated Framework helps organizations evaluate whether internal controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and monitored.

Its five components are:

1. **Control environment:** Establish accountability, ethical expectations, authority, and governance.
2. **Risk assessment:** Identify and analyze risks that could prevent business objectives from being achieved.
3. **Control activities:** Establish procedures and safeguards to address identified risks.
4. **Information and communication:** Ensure relevant information reaches the people who need it.
5. **Monitoring activities:** Evaluate whether controls continue to operate effectively.

**Real-world example:**

An organization has a documented employee offboarding procedure. However, terminated employees occasionally retain access to business applications.

A COSO-oriented approach asks more than whether the policy exists. It examines whether responsibility is clear, the risk has been assessed, access removal is enforced, exceptions are reported, and management monitors recurring failures.

COSO helps connect operational controls to management accountability and business risk.

### CMMI — Improving Process Maturity

CMMI provides models and practices for improving organizational capabilities and processes.

In GRC, a CMMI-informed approach can help assess whether activities such as risk assessment, vendor reviews, control testing, and corrective action management are consistently performed and improved.

Consider a simplified maturity progression:

- **Initial:** Activities are reactive and depend heavily on individuals.
- **Managed:** Responsibilities, schedules, and basic procedures are established.
- **Defined:** Standardized processes are implemented across relevant teams.
- **Quantitatively managed:** Performance is measured and analyzed against defined objectives.
- **Optimizing:** Data and root-cause analysis drive continuous improvement.

This is an illustrative maturity scale, not a substitute for an official CMMI appraisal.

**Real-world example:**

A GRC team repeatedly finds that vendors are assessed only when procurement requests an audit.

The team introduces a standard vendor risk assessment process, assigns risk tiers, establishes review frequencies, tracks completion rates, and analyzes overdue reviews.

Over time, vendor risk management becomes a more consistent, measurable, and repeatable process.

## 2. How the Four Frameworks Work Together

The frameworks should not be treated as four independent compliance projects.

Instead, each should contribute to a shared operating model.

| GRC activity | Primary contribution |
|---|---|
| Information security management | ISO 27001 |
| Privacy management | ISO 27701 |
| Enterprise risk and internal control oversight | COSO |
| Process capability and continuous improvement | CMMI |

For example, consider the risk of unauthorized access to customer information.

- **ISO 27001** helps establish and manage the information security controls.
- **ISO 27701** helps address the privacy implications and applicable personal information protection requirements.
- **COSO** helps establish accountability, management oversight, and monitoring.
- **CMMI** helps standardize access management, measure performance, and improve recurring weaknesses.

The result is a connected process in which security, privacy, control effectiveness, and operational maturity support the same business objective.

## 3. Implementing the Integrated GRC Model: A Practical Roadmap

### Step 1: Define governance and scope

Start by identifying the business units, applications, cloud environments, locations, third parties, and information-processing activities that fall within scope.

Establish clear responsibilities for:

- Executive management.
- Information security.
- Privacy.
- IT operations.
- Business and application owners.
- Risk and compliance.
- Internal audit.

**Artifacts to create:**

- ISMS/PIMS scope statement.
- GRC governance charter.
- Roles and responsibilities matrix.
- Legal and regulatory obligations register.
- GRC reporting and escalation structure.

### Step 2: Perform a combined gap assessment

Assess existing practices against the applicable ISO requirements, internal control expectations, and desired process maturity.

Do not simply record whether a policy exists. Determine whether the policy is implemented, whether evidence is available, and whether the process produces the intended result.

**Artifacts to create:**

- ISO 27001 gap assessment.
- ISO 27701 gap assessment.
- Existing control inventory.
- Personal information inventory.
- Process maturity assessment.
- Remediation roadmap.

### Step 3: Create one integrated risk register

A fragmented risk register creates duplicate work and inconsistent risk ratings.

An integrated register should capture security risks, privacy risks, business impact, existing controls, residual risk, accountable owners, and treatment actions.

A practical example:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Risk ID | RISK-SEC-001 |
| Risk description | Unauthorized access to customer records |
| Business impact | Privacy exposure, regulatory consequences, reputational damage |
| Existing controls | IAM, MFA, access reviews, logging |
| Control owner | IAM Manager |
| Residual risk | Based on the approved risk methodology |
| Treatment action | Improve leaver access revocation and monitoring |
| Due date | Approved risk-based deadline |
| Evidence | Access logs, tickets, review records |

Security and privacy risks can be recorded in a common system while retaining distinct assessment criteria where necessary.

### Step 4: Build a unified control matrix

A unified control matrix connects risks, controls, framework requirements, evidence, and testing.

For example:

| Control attribute | Example |
|---|---|
| Control ID | IAM-001 |
| Risk addressed | Unauthorized access |
| ISO mapping | Applicable ISO 27001 access-control requirements |
| Privacy mapping | Applicable ISO 27701 privacy and access-control requirements |
| COSO mapping | Risk assessment, control activities, monitoring |
| Process objective | Consistent employee access lifecycle |
| Control owner | IAM Manager |
| Frequency | Each relevant access event and scheduled review |
| Evidence | Approvals, access logs, tickets, review records |
| Testing | Sample terminated employees and verify access removal |
| Finding management | Record exceptions, assign remediation, retest |

Mappings should be validated against the actual edition of each standard and the organization's defined scope. Not every control applies to every organization.

### Step 5: Establish control testing and evidence management

A control is not effective merely because a policy has been approved.

The GRC team must evaluate both control design and operating effectiveness.

For an access review, for example, the auditor should determine:

1. Whether the review is formally defined.
2. Whether it is performed at the required frequency.
3. Whether the reviewer has appropriate authority.
4. Whether exceptions are identified and tracked.
5. Whether inappropriate access is removed.
6. Whether remediation is verified and retained as evidence.

Evidence should be traceable to the relevant control, testing period, sample, result, and corrective action.

This makes internal audits more efficient and reduces repeated evidence requests.

### Step 6: Use CMMI-informed maturity assessments

Once core controls are operating, assess how consistently the GRC processes perform.

Possible indicators include:

- Percentage of controls tested on schedule.
- Number of overdue corrective actions.
- Percentage of leaver access removals completed within the approved SLA.
- Percentage of vendor assessments completed by their due dates.
- Number of repeat audit findings.
- Average time required to provide audit evidence.

Establish a baseline before setting targets. Then analyze performance trends and recurring root causes.

The objective is not to achieve a high maturity score on paper. It is to build processes that consistently produce better outcomes.

### Step 7: Establish ongoing assurance

An integrated GRC program should include a coordinated assurance calendar covering control testing, internal audits, privacy assessments, vendor reviews, management reviews, and remediation follow-up.

Management should receive a clear view of:

- Significant risks and changes in exposure.
- Material control failures.
- Privacy incidents and relevant obligations.
- Overdue remediation.
- Audit results and repeat findings.
- Process performance and maturity.
- Resources required to address priority weaknesses.

This connects day-to-day compliance work with management decisions.

## 4. The Most Important GRC Artifacts

A practical integrated program should maintain a controlled set of artifacts rather than creating a separate document for every framework.

Core artifacts include:

**Governance**
- ISMS/PIMS scope and governance charter.
- Information security and privacy policies.
- Roles and responsibilities matrix.
- Legal and regulatory obligations register.

**Risk management**
- Information security risk assessment.
- Privacy risk assessment.
- Integrated risk register.
- Risk treatment plan.
- Risk acceptance records.

**Control management**
- Statement of Applicability, where applicable.
- Integrated control matrix.
- Control owner register.
- Control testing workpapers.
- Evidence repository and evidence index.

**Privacy management**
- Personal information inventory.
- Records of processing, where applicable.
- Privacy request register.
- Retention and deletion records.
- Third-party privacy assessments.
- Privacy incident records.

**Audit and improvement**
- Internal audit program and reports.
- Findings and corrective action register.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Management review minutes.
- GRC KPI dashboard.
- Process maturity assessment.
- Continuous improvement roadmap.

Each artifact should have an owner, version, approval status, review frequency, and retention requirements appropriate to its purpose.

## 5. Common Mistakes Organizations Should Avoid

### Mistake 1: Treating certification as the end goal

Certification is an important milestone where required, but an effective GRC program must continue to monitor risks and controls after certification.

### Mistake 2: Mapping frameworks without validating controls

A control mapped to several frameworks is not automatically effective. Test whether it addresses the relevant risks and requirements.

### Mistake 3: Maintaining disconnected risk registers

Separate systems often create conflicting ratings, duplicate actions, and unclear ownership. Integrate reporting while retaining the detail required for different risk types.

### Mistake 4: Closing findings without validating remediation

A completed ticket does not prove that the underlying issue has been resolved. Verify the fix and assess whether the control now operates effectively.

### Mistake 5: Measuring documentation instead of outcomes

The number of policies written is less meaningful than whether access is revoked promptly, high-risk findings are resolved, and privacy obligations are met.

### Mistake 6: Confusing internal maturity assessments with formal appraisals

A team can use CMMI-informed techniques to improve processes without claiming an official CMMI maturity rating.

## 6. What Does Success Look Like?

A successful integrated GRC model enables an organization to answer five important questions:

1. **What can go wrong?** — Risk identification and assessment.
2. **What are we doing about it?** — Controls and risk treatment.
3. **Can we prove that it works?** — Evidence and control testing.
4. **Who is accountable?** — Governance and control ownership.
5. **How do we improve?** — Performance measurement and root-cause analysis.

When these questions are answered consistently, GRC becomes more than a documentation function. It becomes a management capability that supports resilience, accountability, regulatory compliance, and better business decisions.

## Conclusion: From Framework Implementation to GRC Excellence

ISO 27001, ISO 27701, COSO, and CMMI are most valuable when their contributions are connected.

ISO 27001 establishes the information security management foundation. ISO 27701 strengthens privacy management. COSO connects risk and controls with governance and business objectives. CMMI-informed practices help improve process consistency and measurable performance.

The practical starting point is straightforward: establish one integrated risk register, one control matrix, one evidence repository, one assurance calendar, and a continuous improvement process.

**The real measure of GRC maturity is not how many frameworks an organization has implemented. It is how effectively the organization identifies risk, operates controls, demonstrates accountability, and learns from failures.**

That is how compliance evolves into a sustainable GRC operating model.
