# ISO 27001:2022 Meets ISO 19011: How to Turn GRC Documentation into Real Audit Evidence

**From policies to proof: A practical guide for GRC, IT Risk, and Information Security professionals**

In Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), creating policies and maintaining spreadsheets is only part of the job.

The real challenge is demonstrating that security controls are properly designed, implemented, operating effectively, and supported by reliable evidence.

This is where **ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and ISO 19011 work together.**

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 provides the requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS), while ISO 19011 provides guidance for auditing management systems.

When used together, they help organizations move beyond documentation and build a repeatable, evidence-driven approach to information security.

## 1. Start with the ISMS foundation

Before testing controls, a GRC professional needs to understand the organization's business, risks, regulatory obligations, and information security objectives.

Key artifacts include:

- ISMS scope statement
- Context and interested-party register
- Information security policy
- Roles and responsibilities matrix
- Asset and information inventory
- Risk assessment methodology
- Information security risk register
- Risk treatment plan
- Statement of Applicability (SoA)

**Practical example:**

A fintech company identifies privileged access to production systems as a significant information security risk.

The GRC team records the risk, identifies the risk owner, maps relevant controls, defines treatment actions, and establishes the evidence required to demonstrate that access is appropriately managed.

The objective is not simply to maintain a risk register. It is to connect each risk to a treatment decision and verifiable control evidence.

## 2. Translate ISO 27001 requirements into control evidence

One of the most common challenges in GRC is the difference between having a policy and proving that a control operates effectively.

Consider privileged access management.

Relevant ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A controls may include:

- A.5.15 — Access control
- A.5.18 — Access rights
- A.8.2 — Privileged access rights

The specific controls selected should be justified through the organization's risk assessment and Statement of Applicability.

To assess their implementation, the auditor might request:

- Privileged user account exports
- Approved access requests
- Periodic access review records
- Joiner-mover-leaver tickets
- Authentication and MFA configuration evidence
- Logs showing access revocation
- Evidence of remediation for identified exceptions

The auditor then validates the evidence against defined criteria.

For example, if an employee has privileged access but no retrievable approval record, the auditor investigates whether authorization exists, whether the access is appropriate, and whether the process is operating as intended.

The conclusion should be based on evidence—not assumptions.

## 3. Apply ISO 19011 to conduct a structured audit

A professional audit requires more than asking control owners whether a process is working.

A structured audit typically includes the following activities:

**Step 1: Audit planning**

Define the audit objectives, scope, criteria, timeline, resources, and responsibilities.

**Step 2: Evidence request**

Prepare a request list specifying the required artifacts, reporting period, system scope, and evidence owner.

**Step 3: Audit testing**

Inspect records, interview responsible personnel, observe processes, and test selected samples.

**Step 4: Evidence evaluation**

Compare the evidence with the applicable requirements and internal control criteria.

**Step 5: Findings and reporting**

Document the criteria, condition, supporting evidence, risk implications, and conclusion.

**Step 6: Corrective action and follow-up**

Evaluate root causes, track remediation, validate implementation, and perform retesting where appropriate.

This approach improves consistency, traceability, and the quality of audit conclusions.

## 4. Document findings that management can act upon

A weak audit finding says:

“Access review documentation is missing.”

A stronger finding explains what was tested, what was expected, what was observed, and why the issue matters.

For example:

**Criteria:** The approved access management procedure requires periodic review of privileged access.

**Condition:** During testing, two sampled privileged accounts had no retrievable evidence of review or approval.

**Risk:** Unnecessary or unauthorized privileged access may remain undetected.

**Recommendation:** Validate the business need for the accounts, obtain appropriate authorization, remove unnecessary access, and strengthen evidence retention and review procedures.

**Corrective action:** The responsible owner documents the root cause, defines remediation actions, and establishes a completion date.

**Closure validation:** The auditor examines updated permissions, approval records, and retest results before deciding whether the finding can be closed.

The finding classification should reflect the applicable audit criteria, the extent of the issue, and its significance—not an arbitrary assumption that every documentation gap is a major nonconformity.

## 5. Build an evidence-driven GRC operating model

A mature GRC function should connect its artifacts rather than maintain isolated documents.

A useful traceability model is:

**Requirement → Risk → Control → Artifact → Evidence → Test Result → Finding → Corrective Action → Closure Evidence**

For example:

- Requirement: applicable access control criteria
- Risk: excessive privileged access
- Control: authorization and periodic access review
- Artifact: access review register
- Evidence: account export and signed review records
- Test result: exception identified
- Finding: review evidence missing for selected accounts
- Corrective action: validate access and improve review procedures
- Closure evidence: updated records and successful retesting

This creates an audit trail that another auditor or management reviewer can independently follow.

A master artifact register can track control owners, evidence locations, reporting periods, review dates, test results, finding IDs, and remediation status.

## 6. Measure control effectiveness—not document volume

Having hundreds of policies does not automatically mean an organization has an effective ISMS.

Useful performance indicators may include:

- Percentage of scheduled access reviews completed on time
- Number of overdue high-risk remediation actions
- Percentage of critical vulnerabilities remediated within approved timelines
- Number of recurring audit findings
- Percentage of corrective actions validated as effective
- Completion rate of planned internal audits

Metrics should have clear definitions, accountable owners, reporting frequencies, and meaningful targets.

The purpose is to help management understand whether risks are being controlled and whether the ISMS is improving.

## 7. Common mistakes GRC professionals should avoid

**Mistake 1: Treating the SoA as a checklist.**

The SoA should reflect the risk treatment decisions, applicability, and implementation status of the selected controls.

**Mistake 2: Accepting screenshots without validation.**

Evidence should be relevant, attributable, sufficiently complete, and appropriate to the test being performed.

**Mistake 3: Closing findings based only on management confirmation.**

Verify remediation and, where appropriate, test whether the corrective action is effective.

**Mistake 4: Using the same generic checklist for every organization.**

Tailor the audit program to the ISMS scope, business processes, applicable requirements, and risk profile.

**Mistake 5: Confusing compliance with security effectiveness.**

A documented process may exist while the underlying control still fails. Evaluate both implementation and operation.

## Conclusion: Build a career around execution, not documentation

For GRC and IT Risk professionals, understanding ISO clauses is important—but the ability to execute an audit independently is what turns theoretical knowledge into practical capability.

Learn to define audit scope, map risks to controls, request the right evidence, perform defensible testing, write clear findings, identify root causes, and validate corrective actions.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 establishes the ISMS requirements. ISO 19011 guides the audit approach. Together, they provide a foundation for building a structured, evidence-driven GRC practice.

**The real value of GRC is not how many documents an organization maintains. It is how confidently the organization can demonstrate that its risks are understood, its controls are working, and its weaknesses are being corrected.**
